# bathtub-brush research (WebSearch, 2026-10)
- Bristle stiffness is often color-coded: white soft, yellow or green medium, red or black heavy duty. Start with the softest that works and step up only if grime persists.
- Acrylic and fiberglass tubs scratch easily; soft bristles or non-scratch pads (microfiber, polyurethane) are advised. Very stiff bristles suit cast iron, porcelain-on-steel or stone, and grout lines.
- Handle reach: a standard 20-inch handle suits most tubs; a 51-inch pole reaches across a 60-inch tub while standing. One guide suggests 45 inches or more for standing; a 28-inch handle forces leaning over the rim and strains the lower back.
- Telescoping poles: twist-lock collars can slip when wet; push-button or cam locks and aluminum or thick plastic poles are favored.
- Swiveling head with lockable angles (for example 0, 45, 90 degrees) follows the tub curve and cuts wrist strain. Replaceable pads or heads lower long-term cost.
- Rotating, ergonomic handle shapes are marketed for reduced grip strength or arthritis; these are manufacturer claims.
- Grout: medium-stiff nylon is the safe default. Steel bristles can scratch glazed tile and leave rust marks; brass is gentler than steel but still risky on delicate surfaces. Unsanded grout and natural stone need softer bristles.
- Test any brush on a small hidden spot for about a minute first. Sweep or vacuum first so loose grit is not ground in. Baking soda paste handles general grime.
- Soft brush heads are easier on hands; very stiff small brushes demand more grip force and cause hand fatigue.
