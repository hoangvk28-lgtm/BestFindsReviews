# adaptive cutting board research

- Spiked adaptive boards use stainless or aluminum pins to hold produce, bread or meat in place so one hand can slice or peel.
- A raised corner or rear fence stops food sliding off and gives bread a stop for one-handed buttering.
- Corner guards also work as a barrier for round items such as grapes or olives that roll.
- Suction cup feet suit smooth, dry counters; they lose grip on textured, wet or dusty surfaces, where rubber or silicone feet may hold better.
- Some premium therapy boards add a pivoting knife bracket with three spikes, which is a much higher cost tier than typical spiked boards.
- Retailer "OT recommended" seals are the seller's own programs, not independent certification.
- Check whether spikes are removable or recessed: fixed pins are harder to clean and need care when wiping.
- Plastic (polypropylene/HDPE) is the one common material generally safe in a dishwasher; wood and bamboo are usually hand-washed.
- Bamboo that sits in water can warp or damage its finish; do not soak it.
- Wood and bamboo benefit from occasional food-grade board oil.
- Deep knife grooves in plastic are the main hygiene risk because they are hard to clean by hand; replace badly scarred plastic boards.
- Hot water and detergent removes bacteria effectively on wood and plastic alike; no study has proven plastic is clearly safer than wood.
- USDA-style guidance: keep a separate board for raw meat, poultry and fish apart from boards for bread, vegetables and ready-to-eat food.
- Sanitizing options commonly listed: dish soap and hot water, a 1:1 white vinegar and water mix, or diluted bleach on non-porous plastic.
- Therapists assess grip, tremor and range of motion individually, so product choice is personal and not a medical recommendation.
- Small boards (about 8 inches) are portable but limit what can be cut; larger ones (about 36 x 26 cm) need more counter room.
