# Research: wireless tv headphones (ease of use, not medical)

- Three link types. Proprietary 2.4 GHz RF (digital, often frequency hopping) from a base transmitter to a dedicated headset: no pairing menu, usually the most stable sync and the longest range, but the headset only works with its own base. Bluetooth transmitter plus headset: pairs once, can often also pair to a phone, latency depends on codec. Infrared: line of sight only, can drop if you turn your head or an object blocks the beam, mostly older or budget two-channel sets.
- Latency and lip sync: plain Bluetooth SBC can add 200 ms or more, which is visible as mouths out of step with voices. Low-latency codecs (aptX Low Latency, aptX Adaptive, LE Audio) bring it to roughly 40 to 50 ms, and a sync target of about 70 ms or less is commonly cited as comfortable. Many RF sets claim near-zero delay. Makers publish these figures, so treat them as listing claims.
- Both the transmitter and the headset must support the same low-latency codec; one side only falls back to SBC.
- TV audio outputs: optical (TOSLINK, digital), 3.5 mm headphone/AUX, and red/white RCA analog. Newer TVs often drop RCA or the 3.5 mm jack, so check the back panel before buying. Optical outputs on some TVs only pass certain formats; set the TV audio output to PCM or stereo if the transmitter is silent. HDMI ARC can add delay before the wireless hop starts.
- Using the TV's own Bluetooth audio out: many smart TVs can pair a headset directly, but support varies by model and year, some menus disable the TV speakers or limit the number of devices, and TV Bluetooth sync can lag. A transmitter on the optical or 3.5 mm output avoids that.
- Soundbars: a transmitter fed from the TV's optical out can sit alongside the soundbar only if the transmitter has an optical bypass or the TV outputs to both; otherwise plugging in the transmitter may mute the soundbar. Check this on the listing.
- Range: Bluetooth class 2 is rated around 10 m in the open and drops through walls; RF sets advertise 100 ft and up. Range in a real home is lower than the printed number.
- Battery and charging: stated runtimes range from about 15 to 65 hours per charge. A transmitter that doubles as a charging dock is easiest, since the headset is charged every time it is put away. Check whether charging is by contacts (dock) or a cable.
- Controls: a separate headset volume knob or large physical buttons are easier than app menus or tiny touch panels. Dedicated mute and an EQ or speech mode are useful. Volume on the headset is independent from the TV speakers in dedicated systems.
- Form factors: over-ear (comfortable, warm), on-ear, open-ear earbuds or earpieces (light, can be fiddly), neckband sets, and under-chin/stethoscope-style listeners. Weight in ounces and padding matter for long sessions.
- Two listeners: look for a transmitter that supports two headsets at once (dual-link Bluetooth or a two-headset kit with individual volume), and check whether a second headset is included or sold separately.
- Dialogue features: clear voice or speech EQ modes boost the mid frequencies where speech lives; they help with fast talk or accents but are not a medical device. Anyone with hearing concerns can ask a doctor or audiologist.
- Safety: avoid running listening volume loud for hours; set the headset a little below the level you would use on TV speakers.
