# Research: motion sensor night light (ease of use, not medical)

- Most motion lights use PIR (passive infrared), which reacts to moving heat sources such as a person. Check detection angle (commonly about 120 degrees) and range (commonly 3 to 5 m / about 10 to 16 ft indoors; listings run from about 5 to 25 ft).
- PIR senses side-to-side movement across its field better than walking straight toward it; aim it across the path of travel.
- Dusk-to-dawn (photocell) sensors respond to ambient light, not movement; they glow all night in the dark. Motion lights add a timer (commonly 20 to 90 seconds) after movement stops.
- Plug-in is the lowest maintenance and cheapest to run but needs an outlet at a useful height. Battery or rechargeable suits closets, stairs, under cabinets; higher mAh means longer run time, and recurring battery cost is real.
- Typical night light output is around 10 to 50 lumens; stairs and task spots may want more. Dimmable models avoid glare in a small bedroom.
- Warm white (2700K to 3000K) is gentler at night than cool white, which looks bright in a store but is harsh at 2 a.m. Amber is the lowest glare option.
- Sensor placement: mount near the door in a bathroom so light starts as you cross the threshold; stairs need coverage at top and bottom, not one mid-flight sensor.
- Mounting too high can miss small pets or low movement; furniture blocking line of sight reduces detection.
- Hold time of about 30 seconds or more avoids the light dropping out mid-trip.
- Photocells can be fooled by nearby bright lights, so a light may stay off in a lit room (that is by design) or fail to trigger if a hall lamp is on.
- Adhesive and magnet mounts: magnets need a metal surface or the included metal plate; adhesive reliability matters on textured walls.
- Hard-wired or flush-mount fixtures need an electrician or comfort with wiring, unlike stick-on lights.
